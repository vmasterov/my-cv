After months of job hunting, I put together a few takeaways that might save time and nerves for anyone in a similar situation.

- Don't rush to leave your current job. Test the market first: make sure the roles you want actually exist and the pay matches your expectations.
- If you've suddenly found yourself without a job, accept that the search will be tough (link to previous posts).
- While posting your resume on job sites, send it to your colleagues right away. The recruiters I spoke with during screenings said they give priority to referred candidates.
- If you want to try freelancing, it's easier to do while you still have a steady income. Start by signing up on freelance platforms and seeing how this kind of work actually goes. You may end up disappointed.
- I don't have a recipe for the perfect resume. For experienced developers, my advice is to describe your experience honestly and not let AI write everything for you: give it your key points, then check and edit the wording it suggests.
- It's harder for developers without experience. Companies are reluctant to consider them, and they get stuck in a vicious circle. It's always been this way, but it feels sharper now. To get a foothold in the profession, it's better to play it straight: build a portfolio, network, and do internships. It takes longer and won't pay much at first, but it gives you a solid foundation.
- If you've never done live coding before, a week between the first HR call and the technical screening is unlikely to be enough practice. It's better to solve 2–3 problems a day on a regular basis, so these tasks don't trip you up in interviews.

The IT market will keep changing, and those who can adapt will stay in the game. It's unpleasant, but natural.

I'd like to believe hiring won't turn into a battle between AI writing resumes and AI screening them. And that mentors won't become the only go-between for candidates and employers, the way real estate agents have in big cities.
